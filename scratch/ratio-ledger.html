The Ratio Ledger

Everything the five-hour clip actually taught — the twenty-four ratios, Du Pont, and the operating/cash cycle — walked through on one company so the numbers mean something. This topic has zero scheduled lecture hours; the clip is the whole class.

Vocabulary before formulas

Sixteen words that build all twenty-four ratios

Almost every ratio below is a recombination of these. Learn the words once and the "24 ratios" stop being 24 separate things to memorise.

EPS — Earnings per Share
Net income sliced per share. How much profit one share represents.
BVPS — Book Value per Share
Equity (assets minus debt) sliced per share — what a share is worth on the accounting books, not what it trades for.
P/E ratio
How many times EPS investors are willing to pay for the stock. High P/E means the market is paying a premium, usually for growth it expects later.
M/B ratio — market-to-book
Same idea against book value instead of earnings. High M/B means the market thinks the firm is worth more than its accounting value shows — brand, growth, intangibles.
ROE — Return on Equity
For every THB1 of shareholder money in the firm, how many cents of profit came out.
ROA — Return on Assets
Same question against total assets — ignores how the firm is financed.
Current ratio
Can short-term bills be covered with short-term stuff owned? Above 1× means yes, on paper.
Quick ratio — acid-test
Same question, but inventory is stripped out first — can bills be paid fast, without needing to sell stock?
TIE — Times Interest Earned
How many times over operating profit (EBIT) could cover the interest bill. Low TIE means one bad year from missing a payment.
Debt ratio
What percentage of total assets is funded by debt rather than equity.
Debt–equity ratio — D/E
Same information, reframed: debt as a multiple of equity instead of a percentage of assets.
Equity multiplier — EM
Assets ÷ equity. More debt pushes this higher, which amplifies ROE — for better or worse.
Basic Earning Power — BEP
EBIT ÷ total assets. ROA's cousin, but blind to tax and interest — a pure operating-efficiency number.
Gross / operating / net profit margin
What percentage of revenue survives after, respectively: just the direct cost of making the thing, operating costs too, or literally everything.
TAT — Total Asset Turnover
Revenue squeezed out per THB of assets. An efficiency measure, not a profitability one.
The one identity worth more than all the others ROE = NPM × TAT × Equity Multiplier — the Du Pont breakdown further down. Every "which efficiency is weak" question is this equation in disguise. Separately: ROE, P/E and M/B are linked through EPS and BVPS — since ROE = EPS ÷ BVPS, it follows that M/B = P/E × ROE. Given any two of the three, the third is one line of algebra away.

Two things the exam tests here

One: APT and APD move in opposite directions for liquidity — a high payables turnover is bad news, a long payables period is good news. Same fact, two framings, both examinable.
Two: ART, ARD, IT and ID reappear under asset management below with identical formulas but a different question being asked — here it's "how fast does this become cash?"; there it's "how well is this asset being managed?" Read which family the question sits under.

If a question hands you any one of the three leverage ratios, you have all three without opening the balance sheet.

FCC's numerator and denominator both carry the lease payment

FCC₀₁ = (266 + 28) / (66 + 28) = 294/94 = 3.13×. Leases go on both sides: into the numerator because EBIT was struck after deducting them, into the denominator because they're a fixed charge that must still be met. Add them to only one side and the ratio is wrong. The full formula also grosses up preferred dividends and sinking-fund payments by 1/(1−T) since those are paid from after-tax money — Plow has neither, so the simple version works here.

Family (d)

Profitability — what does it earn, and on what?

Three margins peel back the income statement layer by layer; three "return on X" ratios ask the same profit against a different base.

BEP vs. ROA is the distinction worth holding onto Both divide by total assets. BEP uses EBIT, so it's blind to financing and tax — the honest way to compare two firms with different capital structures. ROA uses net income, so leverage and tax are already baked in. Comparing a levered firm against an unlevered one? BEP is the ratio that answers it.

Tying three families into one number

Du Pont

The most likely constructed-response question in this topic — it forces liquidity, leverage and profitability to be used together.

ROA = NPM × TAT = net incomesales × salestotal assets
ROE = ROA × EM = net incomesales × salestotal assets × total assetscommon equity

Sales cancels against sales, total assets against total assets — net income over equity is what's left.

Worked on Plow, 20X1

ROA = NPM(4.00%) × TAT(1.50) = 6.00%  —  direct check: 120/2,000 = 6.00% ✓
ROE = 6.00% × EM(2.22) = 13.33%  —  direct check: 120/900 = 13.33% ✓

Same for 20X0: 4.56% × 1.70 × 1.91 = 14.80% ✓

Why it matters — five firms, one ROE

Five firms in the same industry. Every one earns exactly 24% ROE. Nothing else about them is alike.

FirmROENPMTATEMWhat kind of business this is
A24%2%6×2×Thin margins, huge throughput — a discount retailer
B24%12%2×1×Fat margins, zero debt — the safest of the five
C24%3%2×4×Mediocre operations, rescued by borrowing
D24%4%3×2×Balanced — nothing extreme anywhere
E24%4%1×6×Assets barely turn over — the entire return is leverage
The point in one sentence: ROE tells you the size of the return, nothing about where it came from or what it cost in risk. B and E post the same number. B earns it from the business; E borrows six times its equity to manufacture it, and a bad year that dents EBIT takes E's equity down with it. If an exam question gives two firms with equal ROE, decompose before judging — that's almost always the answer it wants.
The part that computes cleanly — and feeds the group project

Operating cycle & cash cycle

Everything above measures a snapshot. This measures a loop: how many days cash is tied up between paying for materials and collecting from the customer.

OC = ID + ARD
CC = OC − APD = ID + ARD − APD
PeriodWhat it measuresFormula
Inventory days (ID)Average time to turn raw materials into finished goods and sell themD × inventoryCGS
Receivable days (ARD)Average time to convert a receivable into cash after a saleD × receivablescredit sales
Payable days (APD)Average time between buying materials/labour and paying cash for themD × payablesCGS
Operating cycle (OC)Purchase of materials → collection of the receivable the sale generatedID + ARD
Cash cycle (CC)Payment for materials/labour → collection of that receivableOC − APD
ID and APD divide by cost of goods sold; ARD divides by sales

Inventory and payables sit on the books at cost; receivables sit at selling price. Using sales in the inventory formula is the single most common way to get this wrong — read which base the question hands you and stay consistent with it. Day-count also varies by question: 360 in the Plow tables above, 365 in the examples below. Use whichever the question states.

Two worked examples

John & Sons

50 days to produce and sell, 40 days to collect, 30 days of supplier deferral.

OC = 50 + 40 = 90 days
CC = 90 − 30 = 60 days

The wrap-up section

Eight limitations of ratio analysis

Good MCQ distractor material — several are phrased as questions in the original notes, which is a hint they get asked back.

  1. Comparative ratios are hard to developA firm operating across several industries — which industry average even applies?
  2. "Average" is not a goalHitting the industry average means being unremarkable, not being healthy.
  3. InflationDistorts every balance-sheet figure carried at historical cost, and every ratio built from one.
  4. SeasonalityA balance sheet is one day. A retailer measured the week after Christmas looks like a different company than in November.
  5. Window dressingTransactions timed right at year-end purely to flatter the closing statements.
  6. The notes to the statements matterDifferent inventory or depreciation policies make two identical firms look different on paper. The notes aren't optional reading.
  7. "Good" and "bad" don't generaliseA very high current ratio — good? Or just idle assets earning nothing? Depends what you're asking.
  8. The net effect of a set of ratiosIndividual ratios often point in opposite directions. Judgement is required — which is exactly what the exam is testing.
Plow Chemical, summarised Every family moved the wrong way in 20X1. Liquidity fell (current ratio 2.77→2.33). Assets turned over more slowly (TAT 1.70→1.50) while inventory grew 40% and receivables 11% against sales growth of only 5%. Leverage rose sharply (debt ratio 47.6%→55.0%, debentures +400%), interest expense jumped 40%, and TIE fell from 5.62× to 4.03× — under the industry's 6×. Margins slipped and ROE fell from 14.80% to 13.33%, now under the industry's 15%. The cash cycle stretched by more than eight days.

Diagnosis: Plow funded a large expansion in fixed assets and inventory with debt, and sales haven't arrived to justify it. The market noticed — the share price fell from THB30.75 to THB28.25 while P/E stayed roughly flat, meaning the whole decline is earnings, not sentiment.
